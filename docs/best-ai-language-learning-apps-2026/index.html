You've probably been here before: you download a language app in January, feel great for two weeks, and by March you're staring at a guilt-inducing streak reminder while your actual speaking ability sits exactly where it started.

The problem usually isn't you. It's that most language apps were built to keep you tapping, not talking. The good news is that the newest generation of AI features — real conversation practice, pronunciation scoring, personalized review — has finally started closing the gap between "using an app" and "using the language."

The bad news? Every app now slaps "AI-powered" on its marketing page, and the difference between genuine AI conversation practice and a glorified flashcard deck is enormous. That's what this guide sorts out.

Who this article is NOT for

Let's save you some time. Skip these apps entirely if:

  • You're already at an upper-intermediate (B2) level or higher. Every app on this list caps out somewhere between A2 and B1/B2. Past that point, you need native content, a tutor, or immersion — not an app.
  • You want to pass a specific exam (JLPT, DELE, TestDaF). These apps build general ability, not exam technique. Get a dedicated prep course instead.
  • You'll only practice when it's fun. An AI conversation partner reduces the friction of speaking practice, but nothing removes the part where you show up several times a week.

Still here? Good. Here's how we picked.

How we chose these apps (3 criteria)

1. The AI has to actually do something. We looked for features that adapt to you — conversation practice that responds to what you say, review sessions built from your mistakes, speech recognition that scores your pronunciation. Static lesson content with an "AI" sticker didn't count.

2. Transparent pricing with a way to test-drive. Every pick offers a free trial, a free tier, or a money-back guarantee. We've noted the real-world prices, including the near-permanent "sales" some of these companies run.

3. Evidence it works past week two. We weighed structured curricula, spaced repetition systems, and what long-term users say in reviews — including the complaints. Every app below has a "where it falls short" section, because they all fall short somewhere.

Now the details — including the parts the marketing pages leave out.

1. Babbel — best overall for structured learning

Typical price: $8.95/month on the annual plan (one language); Lifetime access to all 14 languages lists at $299 but is regularly discounted to $170–200. Free option: 7-day free trial, first lesson of every course free, 20-day money-back guarantee.

Babbel is what happens when actual linguists, rather than gamification designers, build a language app. Lessons run 10–15 minutes, follow the CEFR framework (the European standard for language levels), and teach grammar explicitly instead of hoping you'll absorb it by osmosis.

The AI layer sits on top of that curriculum rather than replacing it. Speech recognition gives you pronunciation feedback on every speaking exercise, a personalized review system resurfaces the exact things you got wrong, and Babbel's AI conversation feature lets you practice scenario-based dialogues — ordering food, checking into a hotel, making small talk — with an AI partner that won't judge your accent.

Where it falls short: The content realistically takes you to low-intermediate (around B1) and stops. The AI conversations are guided scenarios, not open-ended chat — if you go off-script, the rails are visible. And the standard subscription covers one language; you need the Lifetime plan to unlock all 14.

Verdict: If you want an app that treats you like an adult who can handle a grammar explanation, Babbel is the strongest structured option on this list. The Lifetime deal, when discounted, is one of the best per-dollar values in language learning. For pricing details, the catalog's weak spots, and who should skip it, read our full Babbel review.

Pros: - Curriculum designed by linguists, aligned to CEFR levels - Explicit grammar instruction (rare in this space) - Personalized review genuinely targets your weak spots - 20-day money-back guarantee on top of the free trial

Cons: - Caps out around B1 — not for advanced learners - One language per standard subscription - AI conversations are templated scenarios, not free-form

2. Duolingo Max — best for building the habit (and the best free start)

Typical price: Free tier with ads; Super Duolingo $83.99/year; Duolingo Max — the tier with the real AI features — $29.99/month or roughly $168/year. Free option: The free tier is genuinely usable long-term, which no other app here can claim.

Duolingo's superpower has never been teaching efficiency — it's that people actually open the app. Streaks, leagues, and an aggressively passive-aggressive owl produce daily engagement numbers the rest of the industry envies.

The Max tier is where the AI gets serious: Video Call with Lily puts you in spontaneous spoken conversation with an AI character who responds to whatever you actually say — the closest thing on this list to a real conversation partner. Roleplay drops you into scenarios, and "Explain My Answer" gives you AI-generated breakdowns of why your sentence was wrong, which partially fixes Duolingo's oldest weakness (no grammar explanations).

Where it falls short: The gamification cuts both ways — Duolingo's own community loudly hates the "energy" system that limits free-tier practice (nearly half of 11,000+ polled users objected to it). Courses for Japanese, Korean, and Mandarin remain shallow on grammar and writing systems. Content plateaus around A2–B1, and the Max AI features only cover the major languages. Note that Duolingo has been shifting which AI features are Max-exclusive versus free, so check the current in-app lineup before paying.

Verdict: Start free. If you find yourself practicing daily after a month, Max's Video Call is a legitimately impressive speaking tool for beginners — but it's the most expensive subscription here, so make sure the habit is real first.

Pros: - Best free tier in the industry, full stop - Video Call is genuine open-ended AI conversation - Unmatched at building a daily habit - 40+ language courses

Cons: - Max is pricey (~$168/yr) for what plateaus at intermediate - Energy system frustrates free-tier users - Weak grammar depth, especially for Asian languages

3. Busuu — best hybrid of AI and human feedback

Typical price: Premium around $69.99/year; Premium Plus around $83.99/year. Discounts are frequent. Free option: Limited free tier; 7-day trial on longer plans.

Busuu's bet is that AI alone isn't enough — so it combines CEFR-aligned courses (A1 to B2, further than most competitors) with a community of native speakers who correct your writing and speaking exercises. Your Spanish gets checked by an actual Spaniard; you return the favor on their English.

The AI side has caught up in the last couple of years: personalized review sessions, AI conversation practice, and a "Mistake Repair" feature on Premium Plus that rebuilds exercises from your error history. McGraw Hill-certified level tests give you something concrete to show for your progress.

Where it falls short: The community feedback loop has latency — you record a phrase, and corrections arrive hours or days later, which is the opposite of real-time conversation. The 14-language catalog is on the smaller side. And a recurring theme in app-store reviews is friction around subscription renewals and cancellation, so set a calendar reminder before your renewal date.

Verdict: The strongest pick if you want AI efficiency plus the reality check of native-speaker feedback — something no pure-AI app can offer. Goes further up the CEFR ladder (B2) than most rivals.

Pros: - Native-speaker corrections on your actual output - Courses reach B2 — higher ceiling than Babbel or Duolingo - McGraw Hill level certificates - Among the cheapest annual prices on this list

Cons: - Community feedback isn't real-time - Smaller language selection - Reported cancellation/renewal friction — mind your renewal date

4. Pimsleur — best for commuters and audio-first learners

Typical price: Premium (one language) $149.95/year; All Access (51 languages) $164.95/year; Lifetime lists at ~$475 but is frequently ~$299 on sale. Free option: 7-day free trial; subscriptions are shareable with up to 3 family members.

Pimsleur is the oldest method on this list — graduated-interval recall, developed by linguist Paul Pimsleur in the 1960s — wrapped in a modern app. Each 30-minute audio lesson has you listen, pause, and respond out loud, with new material resurfacing at scientifically spaced intervals right before you'd forget it.

This makes it the only app here that works completely hands-free: in the car, on a run, doing dishes. The AI Voice Coach adds pronunciation scoring so you're no longer just hoping you sound right. With 51 languages, it also covers languages the others don't touch.

Where it falls short: The method is one-way — you respond to prompts; nothing converses back. The AI Voice Coach scores set phrases but can't handle follow-up questions or sentences you invent yourself. Dialogues lean dated and touristy (hotels, directions, restaurants), and there's minimal reading, writing, or explicit grammar. It's also the most expensive annual subscription on this list.

Verdict: If your only realistic practice window is your commute, nothing else here comes close. Pair it with a writing-focused tool later; don't expect it to carry you alone.

Pros: - Genuinely hands-free — the only true "commute app" here - Spaced-repetition audio method with decades of track record - 51 languages — the biggest catalog on this list - Family sharing (up to 4 people) softens the price

Cons: - No conversational AI — the method is one-directional - Weak on reading, writing, and grammar - Priciest annual plan here

5. Rocket Languages — best one-time purchase (no subscription)

Typical price: Lifetime, one-time: Level 1 $149.95; Levels 1–3 $449.85 — but 40–60% discounts run almost constantly, so real-world prices land far lower. 60-day money-back guarantee. Free option: Free trial lessons, no credit card required.

Rocket Languages is the anti-subscription pick: pay once, own it forever. The core is interactive audio — podcast-style guided dialogues where hosts break down real conversations — backed by culture lessons and spaced-repetition drills. Speech recognition scores your pronunciation on every single phrase, which forces more speaking than most apps manage.

The 60-day money-back guarantee is the longest on this list, and it's a real one — two months is enough time to know whether the method suits you.

Where it falls short: The voice recognition is the weakest AI implementation here — users report it misjudging accents and working best only in Chrome. There's no AI conversation partner at all. The drills get repetitive ("repetition fatigue" is the recurring review complaint), and the recently updated courses drew criticism for switching to AI narration with choppy audio. Like everything else on this list, it won't take you to an advanced level.

Verdict: Buy it on sale (never at list price — the discounts are near-permanent) if subscription fatigue is real for you and you like the guided-audio format. Test the free lessons first to make sure the voice recognition behaves with your accent and setup.

Pros: - One-time payment — zero subscription creep - 60-day money-back guarantee, the longest here - Forces speaking practice on every phrase - Strong cultural context alongside language

Cons: - Voice recognition is hit-or-miss, especially outside Chrome - No conversational AI features - Drills grow repetitive over time

Frequently asked questions

Can AI apps really replace a tutor? Not yet, honestly. AI conversation features (Duolingo Max's Video Call is the current best) are superb for building confidence and getting reps in without embarrassment — but they still miss subtle errors a good tutor catches, and none of these apps reliably takes you past B1–B2. The realistic play: use an app for daily volume, add human conversation (a tutor or language exchange) once you can hold basic conversations.

Which app is best for Japanese, Korean, or Mandarin? Tread carefully — this is where most of these apps are weakest. Duolingo's Asian-language courses are widely criticized as shallow on grammar and writing systems. Of this list, Busuu (Japanese, Korean, Mandarin) and Pimsleur (all three, audio-first) hold up best, but for these languages more than any other, expect to supplement with dedicated writing-system resources.

Are the "sales" real? Mostly no — Babbel's lifetime discount and Rocket Languages' 40–60% off run almost continuously. Treat the discounted price as the real price, never pay list, and don't let a countdown timer rush you.
